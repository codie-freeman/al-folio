The Experience of my Placement Year

Created on July 29, 2026

2026

I was not originally on an integrated placement course. I decided fairly late that a placement year was something I actually wanted, which meant I came to the process without much of a head start. This is a rundown of how that went, what I would do differently, and a few things I think are worth passing on if you are earlier in the process than I was.

Applying

I applied heavily from October through to January. Roles, cover letters, application forms, the usual grind of it. By January I had lost most of my motivation and stopped applying altogether. It felt like a lot of effort going nowhere.

Shortly after a meeting with my university mentor, where we discussed my shift in focus from applying for placement roles, I heard back from Resolian. I would like to say there was a clever strategy behind the timing, but honestly, it was luck. The lesson I took from it was not really about applications at all, it was about not losing motivation. Applying is inconsistent, and the gap between effort and result can be long enough that it feels like nothing is happening even when something eventually will.

The other thing worth saying is that applying can quietly take over. Between forms, research on companies and the general mental load of it. It’s easy to let other responsibilities slip. Treating the search itself as something to be managed, rather than something that manages you.

Interviews

Interview prep matters, but it is not the whole story. In my interview for the Resolian role, the conversation drifted into music and general interests with the person who would go on to be my line manager. It was not a planned tactic, it just happened. He later told me that was genuinely the deciding factor, he wanted someone he could work alongside for 13 months, not just someone who could answer the technical questions well.

Off the back of that, two things stuck with me. First, being likeable and being yourself counts for more than most interview advice gives it credit for. Second, overconfidence can work against you. Being okay with saying you are not sure about something, rather than bluffing through it tends to land better.

Once You Have the Role

Getting the placement is not the finish line, even though it can feel like one after months of applying. It is easy to get comfortable once the job is secured, and I think that is a mistake. The placement itself is where the actual return on the process shows up, and that only happens if you keep looking for opportunities once you are in it, not just the ones that are handed to you.

Over 13 months at Resolian, working across solid-state and physical properties, I was trained to independent competence across a wide portfolio of analytical techniques, and worked to GMP and ALCOA++ documentation standards on more than twenty sponsor projects. Alongside that core work, a few things stand out as the parts I actively went looking for rather than the parts that were assigned to me:

  • An independent case study into how particle size and processing route affect the surface energy of sucrose, which became a poster at the Analytical Research Forum 2026 and, later, a Cheers award internally. (Full write-up on my projects page.)
  • Identifying an underused feature of the lab’s microscopy software, building a large-image workflow around it, and training three colleagues to use it.
  • Staying involved in things that had nothing to do with the day job description: a STEM Ambassador role through I’m a Scientist, continued Python upskilling in the evenings, and a mentorship I kept running alongside the placement.

None of that was expected of me. It came from treating quieter periods as opportunity rather than downtime, and from not assuming the job description was the ceiling of what the placement could be.

What I’d Tell Myself Before Starting

  • Keep applying even when it feels like nothing is landing. The gap between effort and outcome is often longer than it feels.
  • Do not let the application process eat into everything else. Manage it, rather than letting it manage you.
  • Be yourself in interviews. The technical answers matter, but so does whether someone can picture working alongside you.
  • It is fine to say you are not sure. Overconfidence is more often a liability than an asset.
  • Once you have the role, do not get complacent. Keep looking for opportunities, inside and outside your actual job description.

Thirteen months in, this has shaped how I think about the rest of my degree more than I expected it to going in.

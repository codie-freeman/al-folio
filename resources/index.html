Things I wish I had found earlier on whilst at university.

I’m a final-year Pharmaceutical Chemistry student at Reading. I spent a placement year in a GMP lab, working on cheminformatics for my final-year project. This is the list I’d have wanted a year or two ago, what I actually used and why. Useful for anyone but with a slight focus for university of Reading Chemistry students.

Professional bodies and events

  • RSC student membership — Costs very little as a student. Pick an interest group or two: CICAG for computational, JPAG for pharmaceutical analysis. The groups are where the webinars, bursaries and small meetings actually live.
  • Academy of Pharmaceutical Sciences — The UK body for pharmaceutical science. Student membership is cheap and the events lean towards formulation and industry.
  • ELRIG — Drug Discovery, their big annual meeting, is free to attend. Volunteering there is a good way to meet people and see how a conference runs from the inside.
  • Analytical Research Forum — An RSC meeting that welcomes undergraduate posters. I presented my placement work there.
  • Nessa Carson’s conference database — A properly maintained list of chemistry conferences with dates and deadlines. Nothing else comes close.

Science communication

  • STEM Ambassadors (STEM Learning UK) — Register, complete the DBS check, and you can pick from outreach requests posted by schools and events near you. My first was surfactants with 240 primary pupils at the East Kent Science Jamboree.
  • I’m a Scientist, Get Me Out of Here — Live text Q&A with school pupils. After a few chats enables you to do the CPD Academy training, makes you better prepared for chats and future engagement.

Mentoring and development

  • Thrive Mentoring (University of Reading) — Matched me with someone who runs a bioscience consultancy. The best question I asked: how would you assess a candidate like me?
  • RSC Mentoring — Matched me with an early-career computational scientist. Ideal for the questions you’d never put to a supervisor, like what the job is actually like on a Tuesday afternoon.
  • RED Award — Reading’s employability award. Its real value is making you sit down and articulate what you’ve done.

Tools

  • Connected Papers — Give it one paper and it maps the literature around it. The quickest route to the five papers you actually need.
  • RenderCV — The LaTeX template behind my CV. Set it up once and tailoring becomes a content job, not a formatting one.
  • al-folio — What this site rwas built from, hosted on github pages redirected through my own domain.
  • VS Code with Claude Code — How I like to do my coding work, not necessarily the best, but what works for me.

Cheminformatics and ML

  • Practical Cheminformatics Tutorials (Pat Walters) — Where I started. Go through them in order; the model-building and validation notebooks are the ones you’ll reopen for months.
  • CafChemTeach (Dr Mauricio Cafiero) — Reading-specific notebooks on PyTorch, Hugging Face and LLM agents. How I got up to speed before the FYP.
  • Practical Cheminformatics (blog) — Read the dataset-splitting posts first. I lost a week learning the hard way what they explain in an afternoon.
  • The Hundred-Page Machine Learning Book (Burkov) — Exactly enough theory to know what your model is doing, and not a page more.
  • OpenADMET CYP450 Challenge — Enter a blind challenge. Nothing teaches generalisation faster than a leaderboard that disagrees with your cross-validation. The full story is here.
  • ChemRadar — New cheminformatics papers in a single feed. One weekly skim beats a dozen journal alerts.
  • My Self-Initiated Python and AI Learning — The long version of this section: the order I did things in and the models I benchmarked along the way.

Applying

  • Cambridge Careers: CVs and Applications — The clearest CV guide I’ve found, and none of it is Cambridge-specific.
  • Raj Sidhu — Short, no-nonsense videos on getting into competitive graduate roles. If you only follow one careers account, make it this one rather than the LinkedIn influencers.
  • RSC Careers Service — Free CV reviews from people who know the chemistry job market. Far fewer students use this than should.
  • How I Approached My CV — My own write-up, LaTeX template included.

Finding jobs

  • LinkedIn Boolean search — Still the method I trust most. Build a query like ("graduate" OR "placement") AND ("pharmaceutical" OR "analytical chemist") NOT "senior", filter to the past 24 hours. You catch roles on the day they go live.
  • Hiring.cafe — Pulls listings straight from company careers pages instead of reposts, and the filters do what they say. Varied success but better than some of the traditional job boards.
  • SchemeScout — Every graduate scheme and placement with its deadline in one place. Not the best for chemistry based schemes.
  • Milkround — The old-school graduate board. A quick look once a week is enough.
